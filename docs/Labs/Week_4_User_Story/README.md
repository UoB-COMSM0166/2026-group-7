# Week 4 Lab: User Stories & Requirements Engineering

<br>

## Summary

</div>

> The primary objective of Week 4 was **translating our game vision into structured agile requirements**. This included identifying core stakeholders, decomposing our design goals into Epics and User Stories, and defining measurable acceptance criteria — establishing the foundational agile mindset that would guide the rest of the project.

## Initial Stakeholder Identification

</div>

Before defining our Epics and User Stories, we conducted a preliminary brainstorming session to identify our core stakeholders. This ensured our requirements were user-centric from day one. At this early stage, we defined three primary groups:

<br>

| Stakeholder | Role | Key Needs |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Target Players** | Primary end-users — casual gamers and narrative enthusiasts | Engaging parkour mechanics, coherent storyline, fair difficulty |
| **Development Team** | Internal stakeholders — designers, artists, and programmers | Clear task definitions, version control rules, testable acceptance criteria |
| **Academic Evaluators** | "Clients" — tutors and lecturers | Adherence to SE principles, complete documentation, dual difficulty levels |

<br>

> **Note:** As the project evolved into later sprints, this initial list was significantly expanded into a comprehensive [Stakeholder Onion Model](../../../docs/assets/Requirements/Onion_Model.png) to include regulators, negative stakeholders, and playtesters.

## Reflection on Requirements Engineering

During Week 4, translating the abstract vision of Park Street Survivor into structured agile requirements on our Kanban board proved to be our first major software engineering challenge.

<br>

#### Epics and User Stories

Initially, our team struggled with granularity. Our first draft of user stories was too broad (e.g., *"As a player, I want to play a parkour game"*). We quickly learned that effective epics must be decomposed into bite-sized, independent user stories. By breaking down our vision into specific mechanics — such as *"As a player, I want to swipe left/right to change lanes so I can dodge obstacles"* — we made task allocation across the team much more actionable.

<br>

#### Acceptance Criteria

We realised that without clear metrics, the Definition of Done (DoD) is highly subjective. Learning to apply the **Given–When–Then** structure forced us to shift our mindset from designers to testers. Instead of vaguely stating *"the player takes damage,"* we learned to define precise, testable conditions for our early prototypes.

<br>

#### App's Context

The most unique challenge this week was fitting our project's context — a psychological, story-driven runner — into standard agile formats. We had to ensure our user stories captured not just technical movement, but also narrative triggers (like dialogue prompts and branching choices that quietly reshape the ending).

<br>

> Ultimately, this week laid our foundational agile mindset. We learned that rigorous requirements engineering is not just administrative work, but a vital tool to keep our creative vision technically grounded.
